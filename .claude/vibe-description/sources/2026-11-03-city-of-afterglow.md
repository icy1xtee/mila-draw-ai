# The City of Afterglow — описание мира (Павел, 03.11.2026)

Дословно, как прислано.

## Overview

This city lives mostly at night. Night here is not dark; it is colored. The city has grown upward
and inward over itself for a century or more, and no building is ever torn down. New ones are
simply built around, above and against the old. As a result, the city reads like geological
strata. Crumbling colonial-era houses sit at the bottom, then mid-century concrete tenements, and
above them glass-and-steel towers that disappear into a low, glowing sky. Every era is still
inhabited at the same time.

The dominant mood is quiet melancholy without despair. The city is tired, worn and damp, but warm.
People here are lonely together.

## Light and Color

The sky is never black. A permanent haze of humidity, smog and reflected light turns it into a
muted plum, violet or rust-orange ceiling. You don’t see stars, only the lit tops of towers fading
into fog.

Neon magenta and rose-red are the city’s signature colors. Advertising light from towers spills
down and stains the old façades below a bruised pink. Rooftops, water tanks and concrete ledges
glow as if permanently under a red darkroom lamp.

Sodium amber is the color of the streets. Old lamps on bent iron arms throw warm, buttery pools of
light onto the oldest walls, making peeling plaster look almost golden.

Cool cyan and acid green belong to the interiors where young people live and gather: LED strips,
back-lit glass blocks and aquarium-like glows.

Tungsten warmth comes from the windows of apartments, thousands of small yellow rectangles stacked
in grids. Each window is a separate life: a kitchen light, a TV flicker, a curtain half-drawn.

Contrast is everything. A typical view has a cold blue-violet shadow mass, a hot magenta mid-layer
and tiny golden points of human presence.

## Architecture: The Layers

### The Old Core (bottom layer)

Two- and three-story houses from the late 19th or early 20th century, with arched windows, wooden
shutters, ornamental cornices and pitched roofs of dark clay tile now furred with moss and soot.

Their plaster is cracked, water-stained and patched with mismatched colors. Some windows are
bricked up, others boarded, but most still have someone living behind them.

They stand in narrow wedges of land, squeezed on all sides by taller buildings, like old teeth
among new ones.

Many are wrapped in scaffolding that went up for renovations years ago and was never taken down.
Bamboo poles, rusted metal frames and tattered green safety netting have become part of the
architecture.

### The Tenement Belt (middle layer)

Concrete residential blocks, 10 to 20 stories, built quickly and densely. Façades are covered with
grey-blue tiles or painted concrete, streaked with decades of rain runoff.

Every window has its own air-conditioning unit bolted on outside, dripping condensation onto the
street. Together they make an irregular mechanical pattern across every wall.

Balconies are enclosed with metal cages, glass or corrugated plastic and turned into extra rooms,
storage or tiny gardens.

Drainpipes, cables, satellite dishes, laundry poles and rusted water tanks climb every surface like
vines.

The buildings stand so close that neighbors could pass objects from window to window. Between them
hang bundles of black cables, sagging across alleys in every direction.

### The Towers (top layer)

Glass and steel skyscrapers whose upper floors vanish into haze. Some have blue-tinted curtain
walls, others are dark and only partly lit.

They carry enormous illuminated advertising screens and neon signage in red and pink, which is the
source of the magenta light washing over the old city below.

Few people from the streets ever go up there. The towers are visible from everywhere and reachable
from almost nowhere.

### Industrial Remnants

Mixed into the residential fabric are old brick warehouses, factories and printing houses, now
converted into lofts, studios, clubs and communal living spaces.

They have huge steel-framed windows with small panes, often with arched tops; exposed red brick
walls blackened with age; cast-iron radiators; heavy wooden floors; and exposed ceiling
infrastructure such as silver ducts, pipes and beams.

Iron fire escapes zigzag down their façades and the façades across from them. People use them as
balconies, smoking spots and shortcuts.

## Street Level

Alleys are narrow and steep, wet even when it hasn’t rained, from AC drips, washed-down market
stalls and the humidity. The pavement reflects every light source in blurred smears of pink,
purple and gold.

Market lanes are covered with a patchwork of tarps, corrugated metal awnings and faded striped
canopies. Rainbow-segmented beach umbrellas shield stalls selling produce, dried goods, cheap
clothing, hardware and street food. At night the stalls are closed under plastic sheeting, crates
are stacked, and a few bare bulbs still burn.

Signage is everywhere: vertical hanging signs, painted shop names, small circular traffic signs,
old enamel plates and hand-written notices taped to doors. Many neon signs are partially broken,
with letters burnt out so that words become fragments and riddles.

Shopfronts glow in unexpected colors, like a pink-lit boutique window in an otherwise grey alley or
a red-lit doorway plastered with torn posters and old flyers.

Street lamps are old, sometimes leaning, with ornate iron brackets and yellow light that buzzes
softly.

## Places

### The Corner Bar Above the Alley

A narrow brick building at a bend in a cobbled lane. Its upper floor projects outward as a wide
bay window, a glass box of many small panes overhanging the street. Inside, people sit around
small tables under warm light, visible from the street like figures in an aquarium. Above the
window, a neon sign in orange-red cursive is partly dead, so only scattered letters still glow.
Regulars have stopped trying to read it and just call the place by what’s left. On the ground
floor are a dark wooden door, a small display window and tall red-lit panels covered with peeling
art posters. Across the street, the rusty railing of a fire-escape landing gives a perfect view
down onto the bar. Locals sit there on summer nights.

### The Loft with the Arched Windows

A high-ceilinged room in a converted warehouse, several stories above the city. One wall is old
exposed brick, the others are painted deep indigo. Four tall arched windows, divided into dozens
of small panes by black iron frames, fill most of the outer wall. Through them the city is a
vertical tapestry of lit windows, amber and white, stretching up into darkness. A dark lampshade
hangs from a long cord over an unmade bed with rumpled blue-grey sheets. There are two low, round
armchairs in dark leather, a tall cast-iron radiator, a large unframed painting of a stormy
seascape leaning on the brick, and a tripod floor lamp like an old film light. An open book lies
on the floor next to a worn rug, as if someone just stood up from reading. The room is lit almost
entirely by the city outside. It is the most typical home in this world: a private space that is
never truly dark and never fully separate from the city.

### The Green Room (Communal Floor)

A former office or workshop floor turned into a shared sleeping and hangout space. The ceiling is
industrial: silver flexible ducts, a huge sheet-metal ventilation box, painted black beams and old
fluorescent fixtures. The walls are dark brick. One partition is a wall of glass blocks back-lit
in electric cyan. Freestanding paper-and-lattice folding screens in the style of shoji glow from
inside like lanterns. A long table under a green LED tube holds an old monitor or synth. Instead
of beds there are mattresses and heavy duvets on the floor, with cushions and tufted square floor
pillows scattered between them in a shared nest. A potted palm by the window catches a spotlight
beam. Tall windows look onto the brick building across the street with its own fire escape, a few
feet away. Everything is tinted green. This space belongs to the city’s young: musicians,
drifters, night workers and students, who share rent, food and floor space.

## Society and Daily Life

Population density is extreme. Privacy is rare, so people value it intensely and show it through
curtains, screens, enclosed balconies and headphones.

Night is the main time of life. Markets, bars, studios and conversations happen after dark.
Daytime is for sleeping, hidden behind blackout curtains.

The upper city and the lower city barely mix. Tower workers pass through in cars or elevated
walkways. Alley people live and die within a few blocks.

Community is informal but strong: shared lofts, neighbors talking across fire escapes, stall
owners watching each other’s goods, the same faces in the same bar windows every night.

Decay is accepted, not fought. Nobody repaints, nobody demolishes. Things are patched, propped up,
wrapped in netting and left to age. Residents find beauty in this.

Lonely but not hostile. You can be alone here without being in danger. Thousands of lit windows
remind you that everyone else is awake too.

## Sensory Details

Sound: constant humming of air conditioners and transformers, buzzing neon, dripping water,
distant traffic like surf, muffled music through brick walls, a TV in a neighbor’s window, the
clatter of a shutter being pulled down, rain on corrugated metal.

Smell: wet concrete, rust, frying oil and street-food steam, damp plaster, exhaust, incense from
an old doorway, dust heated by radiators, the ozone of old electrical wiring.

Touch and climate: heavy humidity, warm air even at night, condensation on glass, cold metal
railings, rough brick, sticky worn wooden floors.

## The Rules of This World

- Nothing is demolished; everything is built over or around.
- Light comes from signs, windows and screens, never from the sky.
- Every surface carries history: stains, patches, cables, posters, rust.
- Interiors open onto the city through huge windows, so the city is always in the room.
- The color palette is restricted to magenta, amber, violet, cyan and green, with deep shadow
  everywhere else.
- Signs are often broken, and partial words are part of the city’s language.
- Vertical class divides the city: old and poor below, new and distant above, with the warm life
  of the city happening at the bottom.
